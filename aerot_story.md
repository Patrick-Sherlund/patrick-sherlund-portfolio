AeroT Project Story
Executive Summary
AeroT stands for All-Domain Electromagnetic Radio Operator Trainer Tool. I led AeroT as the senior software engineer and project lead from initial discovery through MVP delivery. The project started in December 2024 from a thesis concept by Major William Oblak at Naval Postgraduate School: use inexpensive commercial-off-the-shelf RF hardware to capture live signal activity, reconstruct that activity inside a virtual training environment, place simulated adversarial receivers into that environment, and show instructors whether a student's transmission would likely have been detected.
The core product problem was not just signal collection. It was feedback latency. Marine Coyote training instructors conducting multi-domain training exercises were teaching electromagnetic discipline, but they could not give students actionable feedback within one hour of an operational incident. Feedback often took up to 48 hours to make it back into the training loop, which meant students had already moved on before they understood the consequence of a transmission. AeroT compressed that loop by detecting live RF activity, running terrain-aware RF propagation analysis, and visualizing adversary detectability while the training event was still happening.
The MVP became a three-part system:
An AeroT field device that collected narrowband and wideband RF data, tracked its own GPS and hardware health, and moved compact messages over a low-bandwidth radio network.
A Command View that received device telemetry and signal captures, let instructors configure devices and simulated enemy receivers, ran Longley-Rice Irregular Terrain Model analysis, and visualized heatmaps, timelines, and adversarial receiver links on a map.
An ATAK plugin backed by an on-device MobileService so instructors in the field could view the same signal analysis directly in Android Tactical Assault Kit without pushing heavy heatmap imagery over the LoRa/XBee transport.
This was a hardware, RF, distributed systems, geospatial, embedded Linux, and tactical UI problem at the same time. The final repo shows that AeroT was not a prototype sketch. It became a multi-process device stack in C++ and Python, a C++/React/PostgreSQL command application, a Java ATAK plugin, a C++/Python RF modeling service, SQLite and PostgreSQL persistence, DTED terrain processing, SoapySDR integration, Digi XBee transport, MQTT/WebSocket messaging, and map-based analysis UX.
After the MVP was field-validated, the ATAK plugin became the focus of the next phase of work, and AeroT grew from an after-the-fact analysis tool into a full instructional loop. Two capabilities drove that shift. Planning Mode added pre-execution RF planning so instructors and students could model candidate friendly transmitter placement, adversary detectability, and friendly-to-friendly connectivity before anyone keyed a radio. Training Session Review added a session-level training loop that automatically collects real analyzed events as they happen, preserves the receiver context they were modeled against, and turns a training period into an immediate after-action review. Together they extended AeroT from "what just happened?" into "what should we expect, what actually happened, and how do we teach it?"

Origin
Major Oblak's thesis concept was the starting point: take real RF activity from a live training environment, place that activity into a virtual representation of the terrain, and simulate adversarial receivers as if they existed at meaningful tactical locations. The concept was powerful because it did not require actual adversary receivers to be physically present. The system could ask a training-relevant question instead: "Given this transmission, this terrain, this receiver location, and this configured transmitter power, would an adversary likely have detected the signal?"
When the concept was brought to the Marine Corps Software Factory, it was still a thesis and product thesis, not a physical implementation or fielded system. We did not initially know how much research, hardware integration, and RF engineering it would require. We scoped it with engineers, product leadership, and Major Oblak as a key stakeholder and sponsor. We also scoped it with the discipline we used on other high-risk products: find the thinnest useful slice that could change a user's behavior, then build toward that slice without pretending the entire research space had already been solved.
From December through February, I led the early discovery and framing work. We met with users and units out of 29 Palms, including training communities involved in joint force fires and multi-domain training exercises. Those sessions clarified that the product did not need to become a classified intelligence platform. It needed to become an unclassified training feedback system that helped instructors show Marines, quickly and visually, why electromagnetic discipline mattered.
The product constraints shaped the architecture immediately:
The product had to remain fully unclassified so it could be used by all relevant instructors and students.
The system had to bring its own network because training environments could not reliably provide internet, cellular service, hardwired networking, or power.
The workflow had to be simple enough for instructors to use during a live training event, not after the event in a lab.
The hardware had to be built from commercial-off-the-shelf components where possible.
The software had to tolerate intermittent transport, low bandwidth, device mobility, field power constraints, and disconnected terrain data.
Final MVP Architecture
The final MVP architecture split responsibility across three cooperating surfaces: the field device, the Command View, and the ATAK plugin.
AeroT Field Device
The field device was the physical RF acquisition node. In the field build, the device was based on a Raspberry Pi 5 with SDRs, GPS, radio transport, local power, antennas, and a Wi-Fi hotspot. The hardware stack evolved over the project, but the final direction used SoapySDR-compatible receivers including RTL-SDR for narrowband collection and LimeSDR Mini 2.0 for wider spectrum collection. The device also used a Digi XBee XR 900 class RF module for low-bandwidth radio communication, an external GPS antenna over USB, and an onboard hotspot so ATAK clients could connect directly to the device.
The final hardware narrative included a Raspberry Pi 5, USB expansion for peripheral capacity, a UPS HAT with two 18650 batteries for short runtime during battery swaps, and a DeWalt battery feeding a regulated 5 V, 10 A step-down converter. The antenna layout had distinct roles: a rigid high-gain antenna for the 900 to 930 MHz XBee transport band, a wider VHF/UHF collection antenna for lower-frequency activity, and a 120 to 512 MHz antenna for line-of-sight frequencies monitored through the RTL-SDR path. The repo validates the software side of those interfaces through SoapySDR, Lime/RTL detection, USB GPS handling, XBee transport, and hotspot setup scripts.
The repo-backed device stack is centered on DeviceManager, a C++ application that coordinates local services. At startup, DeviceManager/src/main.cpp initializes GPS, hardware telemetry, SDR management, LoRa connection handling, process launchers, and a local Drogon WebSocket endpoint on 127.0.0.1:6969. The device launches the Python LoRa client from LoRaDriver and the Python MobileService from MobileService as child services. This gives the device a clean split:
C++ owns SDR control, signal processing, local configuration, device health, and orchestration.
Python owns the Digi XBee API integration because the vendor support is native and practical in Python.
MobileService owns the ATAK-facing API, WebSocket feed, on-device RF modeling, and local persistence needed by the ATAK workflow.
The device stores local state in SQLite through DeviceManager/src/database/DataAccessLayer.cpp. It persists the device UUID, application version, configured transmit power, hardware samples, and configured line-of-sight frequencies. GPS is handled through DeviceManager/src/services/GpsService.cpp, which scans Linux serial devices, reads NMEA data, and parses GGA/RMC sentences into live latitude, longitude, altitude, UTC time, and date. Hardware health is handled through DeviceManager/src/services/HardwareService.cpp, which reads CPU temperature, RAM utilization, and storage utilization from Linux system interfaces.
The field device's job was to collect a baseline of the RF environment, continuously sweep configured frequencies, detect statistically meaningful deviations from that baseline, and emit compact signal capture messages. It also emitted health messages so Command View and ATAK users could see device location and hardware status.
RF Collection and Signal Detection
DeviceManager discovers SDR hardware through SoapySDR in DeviceManager/src/sdrDrivers/SdrManager.cpp. The final code classifies RTL devices as narrowband drivers and Lime/FT601 devices as wideband drivers. That let the architecture support two different sensing behaviors without coupling the rest of the product to a specific SDR model.
The narrowband path is implemented by DeviceManager/src/sdrDrivers/NarrowbandSdrDriver.cpp. It configures an RTL-SDR style receiver with a 2.4 MHz bandwidth and 3.2 MS/s sample rate. Each configured line-of-sight frequency gets its own monitoring state. When more than one frequency is assigned to a receiver, the driver retunes in a round-robin loop, flushes stale samples after each retune, and evaluates each frequency independently. That design is important because it prevents one frequency's baseline from contaminating another frequency's detection statistics.
The wideband path is implemented by DeviceManager/src/sdrDrivers/WidebandSdrDriver.cpp. It supports LimeSDR-style collection, disables transmit behavior on the Lime side, and runs the same general baseline/detection pattern over a wider configured band. The current implementation caps active device bandwidth at 30.72 MHz, which is consistent with the default WideBandConfig sample rate. Command View allows a wider configured range, but the device-side collection path is bounded by the current SDR driver implementation.
Both drivers use SdrDriverBase to build signal capture messages. A signal capture includes the device UUID, signal UUID, timestamp, frequency band bounds, and measured power. The driver emits a START capture when a signal crosses the detection criteria and an END capture when it returns to baseline. Those paired messages let downstream services reconstruct a transmission event rather than treating every power sample as a separate incident.
The signal processing path starts with power spectral density. DeviceManager/src/sdrAlgorithms/PowerSpectralDensity.cpp uses FFTW, applies a Hanning window to IQ samples, performs a forward FFT, and computes an average magnitude-squared power estimate. The FFT size scales with bandwidth, so wider bands get proportionally larger spectral transforms.
The current anomaly detector lives in DeviceManager/src/sdrAlgorithms/AnomalyDetection.cpp. It is more sophisticated than a simple fixed threshold. The detector keeps a rolling baseline of up to 256 power samples. Once calibrated, it estimates a skewed Cauchy distribution over the baseline:
The location parameter is based on the median baseline power.
The scale parameter is derived from the interquartile range.
The skew parameter is estimated by minimizing negative log likelihood across candidate values.
Each new power sample is evaluated using the upper-tail probability of the fitted distribution.
A sample is anomalous when the tail probability is below the configured alpha threshold.
The detector requires ten consecutive anomalous observations before declaring a signal active and ten consecutive normal observations before declaring the signal ended.
That consecutive-sample requirement matters in the field. SDRs can produce transient bad reads during retune, power variation, USB hiccups, or local interference. A one-sample trigger would create noisy false positives. A rolling probability model with start/end debouncing produces fewer false signal events while still remaining lightweight enough to run on the device.
Device Messaging and Low-Bandwidth Transport
The final radio transport is implemented in LoRaDriver/LoRaClient.py. The code uses the Digi XBee Python API, scans serial devices for Digi/XBee-compatible USB descriptors, opens the radio at 9600 baud, sends broadcast and unicast payloads, and bridges messages between the local DeviceManager WebSocket and the XBee network. The shared protobuf messages are defined under Shared/protobuf/proto, including health messages, frequency configuration messages, connect requests, and signal captures.
The main message flow is:
A device sends device.connect.request.
Command View acknowledges and publishes config.frequency.
The device stores the frequency configuration, sets its transmit power modeling parameter, assigns line-of-sight frequencies across RTL receivers, configures wideband behavior if enabled, and starts collection.
The device sends recurring device.health messages with GPS and hardware telemetry.
When the detector sees a configured signal, the device sends signal.capture messages with compact event data.
The final device implementation uses a local WebSocket bridge between C++ DeviceManager and the Python LoRa client. The shared repository also contains MQTT topic wrappers and models, and the Command View side uses Mosquitto for message distribution. This split is a practical example of the project adapting over time: use the message model consistently, but choose the local transport that best fits each runtime boundary.
Command View
Command View was the main instructor and command-node application. It is a full-stack application under _temp/AeroT_CommandViewRepo-main/AeroT_CommandViewRepo-main. The frontend is React and TypeScript with MapLibre, React Query, MUI, and geospatial UI components. The backend is C++20 with Drogon, PostgreSQL, Mosquitto, OpenCV, and shared protobuf/message models. The development stack is composed with Docker using PostgreSQL and Eclipse Mosquitto. In the field architecture, the command node also had its own Digi XBee radio connected to the workstation so it could receive device traffic and publish configuration or acknowledgement messages back to the network.
The backend follows a service-layer architecture. src/DIContainer.cpp wires repositories, services, signal deconfliction, heatmap services, MQTT subscribers, and publishers through dependency injection. Controllers expose HTTP endpoints for devices, device frequency configuration, simulated enemy receivers, heatmaps, and map tiles. The controllers translate request and response concerns, while services own orchestration and business logic.
Command View receives three important live inputs:
Device connection requests.
Device health messages.
Signal capture messages.
ConnectionRequestSubscriber.cpp handles new device connection requests. It creates missing device records and publishes a frequency configuration acknowledgement. HealthSubscriber.cpp updates GPS and hardware status. SignalEventSubscriber.cpp receives signal captures and forwards them into SignalDeconfliction.cpp.
Signal deconfliction is necessary because the network can contain multiple AeroT devices and multiple observations of the same signal. The deconfliction service groups messages by signal UUID, waits for paired START/END observations, compares candidate captures by time and band characteristics, and chooses the strongest relevant observation when duplicate devices report the same signal. It also avoids generating repeated heatmaps for the same device within a short duplicate window. That turns raw detection messages into an instructor-facing event.
Command View also owns configuration. DeviceFrequencyController.cpp validates line-of-sight frequencies, excludes the 900.8 to 929.2 MHz transport band, validates wideband ranges, persists configuration, and publishes frequency updates back to devices. The configured transmit power is intentionally operator-provided rather than inferred from received IQ data. That is a technical limitation of the problem domain, not a missing feature. Without a calibrated transmitter, antenna system, impedance chain, antenna gain pattern, path loss model, and frequency-specific setup, a receiver cannot infer the true original transmitter power from the received signal alone. AeroT uses configured transmit power as an explicit modeling input for analysis.
The frontend centers the user around a MapLibre map. Map3D.tsx, MapUtils.tsx, and the surrounding context providers render AeroT device markers, simulated enemy receiver markers, map tiles, terrain/hillshade sources, and signal analysis overlays. The map stack supports local raster tile endpoints for disconnected use and terrain/hillshade layers for geospatial context. Instructors can place enemy receivers, select devices, inspect health, configure frequencies, and view signal coverage. The timeline component lets users scrub historical signal events, restore the heatmap and receiver-link context for a past capture, and inspect the same event details later, including transmitter context, receiver context, frequency, power, distance, and likelihood.
Longley-Rice Signal Analysis
The Command View's core value is not that it knows a device heard energy. The value is that it turns that detection into a training consequence. The signal analysis service takes a transmission event, a configured transmitter power, device location, simulated receiver locations, and DTED terrain data, then runs Longley-Rice Irregular Terrain Model analysis.
The backend implementation parses DTED terrain, samples terrain profiles between transmitter and receiver points, computes great-circle distances, runs point-to-point Longley-Rice path loss, estimates received power, and classifies whether each simulated receiver could likely detect the signal. The effective model is:
Build a terrain-aware path between transmitter and receiver.
Compute path loss using Longley-Rice ITM.
Estimate received power as transmitter power in dBm minus modeled path loss.
Compare the received power against configured receiver sensitivity thresholds.
Classify receiver detectability as low, moderate, or high.
The same analysis also generates heatmap output. The heatmap code evaluates the signal over a grid, normalizes modeled received power into a visual intensity scale, applies an OpenCV color map, adds alpha, encodes the PNG, and attaches it to a map overlay using the geographic corners of the image. That produces two instructor-facing outputs:
Receiver links from transmitter to simulated adversarial receivers, colored green/yellow/red by detectability.
A georeferenced heatmap overlay showing where the transmission would likely be visible across the terrain.
That is the training moment. The instructor can click a transmitter, click an enemy receiver, see frequency and power context, inspect likely detection, and explain the consequence while the event is still fresh.
ATAK Plugin and On-Device MobileService
The ATAK plugin solved a different operating mode. Command View worked well as a command node, but instructors also needed a field view while geographically separated from the command node. The low-bandwidth XBee/LoRa transport could carry compact protobuf messages, but it could not practically carry frequent PNG heatmaps and heavy analysis artifacts. The solution was to run the analysis close to the device and expose it over the device hotspot.
That is why the final architecture includes MobileService under MobileService. MobileService is a Python aiohttp service running on the AeroT device. It exposes REST and WebSocket endpoints on port 8765, including device state, health subscriptions, frequency configuration subscriptions, enemy receiver CRUD, TX events, and heatmap retrieval. It persists operational state in PostgreSQL, stores heatmaps through MinIO, and calls into C++ RF modeling bindings through MobileService/RadioTransmissionSimulator.
The RF modeling code in MobileService mirrors the Command View analysis path. It defines transmitter sites, receiver sites, ITM configuration, simulation outputs, heatmap configuration, DTED terrain loading, bilinear elevation lookup, terrain profiles, Longley-Rice path loss, received power, detectability classification, and heatmap PNG generation. The intent was consistency: the Command View and ATAK workflow should explain the same signal event the same way.
The ATAK plugin lives under _temp/AeroT_ATAK_Plugin. It is a Java Android ATAK 5.1.0 plugin using MVVM structure, Dagger dependency injection, OkHttp WebSockets, Room persistence, and ATAK map APIs. The plugin connects to ws://aerot-device.local:8765/ws, subscribes to health, frequency configuration, enemy receiver, and signal analysis updates, and uses REST calls for TX events and heatmap assets.
On the map, the plugin overlays the same conceptual objects as Command View:
AeroT device state.
Enemy receiver placement and deletion.
Signal analysis events.
Heatmap PNG overlays georeferenced onto the ATAK map.
Analysis lines from transmitter to adversarial receivers.
Receiver details, event details, and timeline selection.
The plugin uses ATAK-native mechanisms where appropriate. Enemy receivers are created as map items through ATAK placement and marker APIs. Signal coverage heatmaps are rendered through ATAK RubberSheet overlays. Analysis links are rendered as ATAK polylines. The result is not a web UI embedded in ATAK. It is an ATAK-native operational view backed by the same RF analysis model as the rest of AeroT.
Each iteration was roughly a three-to-four-month block, and each block moved the product from concept toward field-validated MVP.
Iteration 1: Proving the Device and Command View Slice
The first iteration had two simultaneous goals. First, we had to prove that we could build a real-time RF collection device from commercial components. Second, we had to prove that instructors could understand and act on the information through a usable command interface.
On the device side, the early build focused on narrowband collection with RTL-SDR. There was no LimeSDR wideband path yet. The transport hardware at this stage used a LillyGo LoRa chip before the project later moved to Digi XBee. This iteration was about getting the hardware APIs, SDR control, baseline collection, device telemetry, GPS, transport, and command configuration flow into one coherent loop.
The Raspberry Pi 5 power behavior became one of the earliest engineering lessons. The device needed stable power under USB peripheral load. In the first hardware build, we used a custom power supply to step a DeWalt battery down to the Pi's expected power profile. When the converter could not regulate cleanly under load, the Pi firmware would throttle peripherals and the device would randomly lose USB-connected components such as SDRs or the LoRa radio. That was not a software bug, but it looked like one until we understood the hardware failure mode. The lesson was simple and expensive: a field RF device is only as reliable as its power path, and the Pi's USB behavior makes marginal power regulation visible as random peripheral failure.
We also learned that the enclosure mattered. The first chassis was 3D printed with ventilation holes and mounted fans. It gave us enough physical structure to start field handling, but heat, airflow, cable routing, antenna placement, and maintainability were already becoming part of the software problem because thermal throttling and peripheral disconnects directly affected collection reliability.
On the Command View side, we built the first version of the instructor map and device interface. Even in the early form, the UI needed to answer practical field questions:
Where are the AeroT devices?
Are they healthy?
What frequencies are they configured to monitor?
What signal events have been detected?
Where would simulated adversarial receivers have seen the transmission?
The first iteration established the product shape: device telemetry, configured frequency monitoring, signal capture messages, a map-centered UI, and the beginning of the analysis workflow.
Iteration 2: Better Power, Wideband Expansion, and KL Divergence
The second iteration hardened the device and expanded collection capability. We moved to a better commercial step-down power converter that had been tested under load and could regulate the power stream without the dips that caused USB throttling. This reduced random peripheral shutdowns and made software behavior easier to reason about because the hardware stopped failing in intermittent ways.
Algorithmically, this is where the signal detector moved beyond a generic baseline anomaly detector into a KL divergence approach. The detector compared baseline distribution behavior against sweep behavior and scored deviations. We calibrated thresholds based on the frequencies and observed environments we had available at the time. When the score crossed a threshold, the device classified the observation as a detection and sent a signal capture to Command View.
This iteration also introduced LimeSDR Mini 2.0 support for wideband collection. Wideband was much harder than narrowband because the device had to process a larger sample stream and larger FFT workload. The repo's current implementation shows the evolved version of that path in WidebandSdrDriver.cpp, where the Lime path runs a PSD and anomaly loop similar to narrowband collection while respecting a 30.72 MHz active bandwidth cap. The Command View configuration model allows wider ranges, but the device-side implementation is bounded by the active SDR sampling path.
Physically, the enclosure also changed. The black case from the first iteration was problematic in sun and heat. We moved to the white "storm trooper" case with better ventilation, better component placement, and a more field-aware internal layout. This was not just an industrial design improvement. It was part of making the RF sensing stack reliable enough to test.
Iteration 3: Self-Interference, GPS, and Digi XBee
The third iteration exposed a subtle field failure: the device could interfere with itself. The SDRs, radio transport, Raspberry Pi, power supply, hats, and cabling were all emitting electromagnetic noise inside a compact enclosure. During testing, we found that the GPS receiver could be effectively denied by our own hardware. When the antenna was externalized away from the build, GPS worked. When it moved back toward the device, GPS degraded or failed.
We investigated the problem with lab testing and frequency probe kits, then tried to reduce emissions by wrapping parts of the case interior with EM tape. That did not fully solve the issue. The lean path to keep the MVP moving was to use a better external GPS antenna and move forward rather than spending the rest of the iteration characterizing every internal emission source. That was the right product call. The MVP needed reliable location, and a better antenna solved the operational problem even if it did not completely close the research question.
This iteration also moved the transport away from the earlier LillyGo approach and toward Digi XBee. The current repo confirms that final direction in LoRaDriver/LoRaClient.py, which uses the Digi XBee API and protobuf message bridge. That change mattered because the transport layer was not just a radio link. It was the backbone for device join, acknowledgement, frequency configuration, health reporting, and signal capture delivery.
By the end of this iteration, AeroT had become more than a software stack attached to a radio. It was a field system where enclosure design, antenna placement, RF emissions, GPS quality, transport reliability, and software behavior all had to be debugged together.
Iteration 4: Hardened MVP, ATAK, MobileService, and Field Validation
The fourth iteration moved the system into its MVP form. The hardware was externalized into a Pelican-style case with a more refined internal chassis. The device detection algorithm moved beyond the KL divergence approach into the current probability-based detector shown in the repo. That final detector uses rolling baseline power samples, skewed Cauchy fitting, upper-tail anomaly probability, and consecutive-sample debouncing to determine signal start and end events.
The largest software addition in this phase was ATAK and MobileService. Command View remained the command-node application, but instructors needed an ATAK-native field view. Because heatmap PNGs and frequent analysis artifacts were too large for the low-bandwidth radio network, the AeroT device had to perform signal analysis locally and expose it over its Wi-Fi hotspot.
MobileService became that on-device backend. It listens on port 8765, maintains WebSocket subscriptions, exposes REST endpoints, persists TX events and receiver state, generates heatmaps, and calls the C++ RF modeling library. The ATAK plugin connects to MobileService, places enemy receivers, displays signal coverage heatmaps, draws detectability links, and lets instructors inspect analysis details inside the ATAK map.
The final validation you described was the proof point: ten AeroT devices connected across roughly ten miles of hot, sandy, mountainous terrain at 29 Palms, with ATAK clients in instructors' hands and Command View running at the command node. The system delivered signal feedback within about five seconds of capture. That changed the training dynamic. Instructors could walk to students immediately and show them that a transmission would have been seen by simulated adversarial receivers in dispersed positions. The consequence was no longer abstract or delayed by two days. It was visible in the moment, attached to the terrain, and tied to the student's own action.
That outcome is the most important part of AeroT. The technical stack mattered because it created a training behavior that did not exist before: immediate, terrain-aware electromagnetic feedback during live instruction.
Beyond the MVP: Planning Mode and Training Session Review
The field-validated MVP proved AeroT's core loop: detect a real signal, model adversary detectability against terrain, and show the instructor fast enough to change behavior. But that loop only spoke to one moment in time, after a transmission had already happened. The next phase of work, centered on the ATAK plugin, widened AeroT across the whole instructional cycle by adding two capabilities that sit on either side of the operational event.
AeroT now has three complementary map workflows, and the discipline of the design was keeping them distinct rather than collapsing them into one screen:
Signal Coverage Maps are operational evidence. They answer: given this actual detected RF event, what did the modeled consequence look like?
Planning Mode is a pre-execution planner. It answers: given these candidate friendly placements, what would likely happen before anyone transmits?
Training Session Review is the session-level narrative. It answers: what happened across the training period, what mattered, and how should the instructor turn it into an after-action review?
Signal Coverage is evidence after the fact. Planning Mode is modeled foresight before the fact. Training Session Review is the story that ties a training period together over time. All three reuse the same terrain-aware modeling philosophy and the same honest, probabilistic language, so a student sees one consistent product rather than three.
Planning Mode: Pre-Execution RF Planning
Planning Mode is AeroT's pre-execution RF planning surface inside the ATAK plugin. The user places candidate friendly transmitter positions on the map, optionally places simulated enemy receivers, configures the RF inputs that matter — frequency, modeled transmit power, and antenna height — and runs an analysis that explains how those positions behave against the modeled RF environment before a real transmission happens.
It uses the same modeling philosophy as the operational Signal Coverage workflow: terrain-aware propagation, configured RF inputs instead of pretending to infer unknowable transmitter truth, receiver-specific outcomes, and language that stays probabilistic and modeled. Planning Mode does not claim a placement is safe or that a real adversary will or will not detect a signal. It makes RF tradeoffs visible so instructors and students can reason about exposure, placement, and communication viability before execution.
The original Planning Mode answered a single question: if a friendly unit transmits from here, can the modeled enemy receiver layout see it? Each candidate placement was evaluated against enemy receiver positions, producing ranked recommendations, a per-card detection summary, and a Placement Detail modal with receiver-specific outcomes.
The friendly-link expansion
The most important Planning Mode expansion widened the question from "can the enemy see me?" to also include "can friendly candidate units still talk to each other?" A low-exposure position is useless if it isolates the unit from the rest of the team, so Planning now models a genuine tradeoff between detection risk and friendly connectivity.
This required a candidate-to-candidate path-loss analysis. For each unique pair of candidate units the backend runs the modeled path loss once — the candidates share the same RF specs and the pair is symmetric for MVP purposes, so there is no need to run A-to-B and B-to-A separately. Friendly link outcomes use a deliberately different vocabulary from enemy detectability: a link is Viable, Marginal, or Unreliable, never "detected." The question for a friendly link is whether a usable link can likely be established, not whether an adversary saw it.
The expansion also relaxed the old rule that enemy receivers had to be present. Planning analysis now runs whenever there is a meaningful path: two or more candidate units with no enemy receivers, so friendly connectivity alone can be evaluated; or one or more candidates with one or more enemy receivers, so detectability can be evaluated and connectivity layered in when multiple candidates exist.
Result cards became two-dimensional. Each candidate now shows a Detection Result — enemy receiver exposure classified as Exposed, Possible, or Clear — and a Connectivity Result — the friendly-link outcome. For connectivity-only scenarios with no enemy path, the card keeps the same structure and uses a "No peer units" / not-applicable treatment instead of collapsing, so card heights and scanning stay stable. The card stays a quick decision surface; it does not become the detail view.
The Placement Detail modal gained a dedicated Friendly Links tab that mirrors the existing receiver tab structure but shows candidate-to-candidate results, and the Assessment tab reuses the existing tick-bar container to show friendly-link quality alongside enemy detectability. This was intentional reuse, not a new visual language. The user already understands the Planning modal's assessment style, and friendly links extend it.
Ranking now weighs both dimensions. Where enemy receivers exist, detection remains primary, preserving the original product value of avoiding exposure; connectivity adds a second axis so a recommended placement is not merely hidden but also able to reach the team. In connectivity-only jobs, the strongest friendly links drive the ranking. This prevents the bad planning outcome of choosing a low-risk position that cannot communicate.
Map lines and layering
Planning Mode draws map geometry lines to explain modeled relationships, and the line rules were tightened so the map shows teaching value rather than clutter. Candidate-to-enemy lines are drawn only when the modeled receiver result is meaningful exposure — Exposed or Possible — and never for Clear, not-applicable, or unknown outcomes. Candidate-to-candidate friendly lines are drawn only when the link is Viable, reusing the existing green/success styling; Marginal and Unreliable links remain available in the detail UI but do not become map lines. The layering rule keeps the base ATAK map at the bottom, the Planning heatmap as context above it, the geometry and link lines above the heatmap, and candidate and receiver markers and selection affordances on top, so the explanatory lines always stay visible over the heatmap.
Candidate heatmaps
The next expansion gave each top recommendation a terrain-aware coverage heatmap, closing the gap between Planning's precise-but-narrow receiver links and the broader RF footprint that Signal Coverage already showed. A heatmap answers a question links cannot: if this candidate transmits from here, what is the broader modeled coverage and exposure footprint across the terrain, including how ridgelines, power, and position shape the signal beyond the placed receivers?
Crucially, Planning candidate heatmaps reuse the Signal Coverage heatmap generation path rather than introducing a second algorithm, color scheme, or modeling product. The work extracted the pure terrain-aware generation path — build the transmitter site, configure heatmap output, resolve the terrain zone, compute the SW/NE overlay bounds, call the C++ heatmap generator, and encode the PNG — so Planning could reuse it while Signal Coverage behavior stayed unchanged.
Two decisions kept this honest and affordable. First, Planning heatmaps are temporary. They are not historical artifacts, not Training Session artifacts, not written to Postgres, and not linked to tx_events. Each new Analyze invalidates the previous Planning heatmap data, stored under Planning-owned planning-* keys with prefix-guarded cleanup that never bulk-clears the heatmap bucket or touches Signal Coverage heatmaps. Second, generation is capped at the top 10 recommendations. Planning can sample many candidates, and generating hundreds of 1024×1024 terrain-aware heatmaps would be expensive and pointless; recommendations beyond the cap keep null heatmap fields and remain valid, they simply have no overlay.
On the client, only the selected recommendation's heatmap is visible. It renders through the same RubberSheet-style overlay path as Signal Coverage, swaps when the selection changes, and clears on a new Analyze, on reset, and on exit. Planning bounds handling is deliberately stricter than Signal Coverage's historical fallback: if bounds are missing, partial, non-finite, or invalid, the client renders no overlay rather than guessing a candidate-centered fallback, because a wrong overlay would misrepresent candidate coverage. The client also guards against stale in-flight requests re-adding an old overlay.
Live heatmap generation progress
Because candidate heatmaps take real time to generate, the loading experience needed to be more honest than a generic "Generating heatmaps" spinner. The backend exposes count-based progress through the existing Planning job status response and planning_job_changed serialization — no new endpoint — reporting totals, completed, succeeded, failed, and skipped counts, the current candidate, and per-item state (PENDING, GENERATING, AVAILABLE, FAILED, SKIPPED). The model is deliberately candidate-level rather than fake per-pixel progress, because the C++ generator is synchronous for a single heatmap; the honest unit of progress is "this candidate is generating, this one finished, this one was skipped."
The frontend was tuned for the narrow ATAK panel: it shows one active candidate at a time and an overall "Generating x of y coverage heatmaps" status with a compact summary like "2 complete · 1 remaining," rather than rendering a full ten-row queue. The progress bar was aligned so that "Generating 3 of 3" visually reads 3/3, the current-candidate readout was reworked from a floating card into an integrated loading row, and the cancel control was restyled as a calm secondary action.
Training Session Review: The Session-Level Training Loop
Training Session Review is AeroT's session-level training loop. An instructor starts a session, the system preserves the receiver context, real analyzed Signal Coverage events attach themselves to the session automatically as they complete, the instructor marks teaching moments along the way, and ending the session produces an immediate after-action review.
The feature exists because AeroT's strongest value was never a single heatmap or one receiver link. It was the training moment, and the instructor needs to reason at the level of the whole session: what happened during the period, which transmissions were most exposed, which simulated receivers mattered, which moments are worth teaching, and whether behavior improved. Without a session feature, the instructor can still inspect individual events, but the story stays fragmented and has to be reconstructed from memory. Training Session Review builds enough structure during live training that the AAR is already formed when the session ends.
Lean MVP Core first
The first implementation was deliberately narrowed to prove the loop before building a reporting system. MVP Core supports one active session per MobileService instance; a setup step with a session name, receiver readiness, a receiver-snapshot preview, and an initial note; a durable snapshot of the receiver layout at session start; automatic attachment of completed analyzed events while the session is active; a live session panel with elapsed time, event count, latest events, and an end action; a persistent active-session chip; event cards and event detail that reuse Signal Coverage's assessment and receiver-outcome concepts; per-event pin and note support for teaching moments; an end-session confirmation; a simple AAR with summary, timeline, teaching moments, receiver-exposure summary, and notes; protection against Signal Coverage's Clear All while a session is active; and modeled language with assumption warnings throughout.
It deliberately defers the north-star pieces: starting a session from a Planning result, durable Planning snapshots, plan-to-execution comparison, manual event attachment, reopening prior ended sessions, export and report packaging, archive filters, offline mutation, and anything resembling numeric grades or automated coaching. That sequencing kept the first version focused on the session loop instead of a heavy report engine.
MobileService as the source of truth
The key architectural decision was making MobileService — not the ATAK client — the source of truth for sessions. Multiple ATAK clients can connect to the same device, events can complete after a client panel has closed, receiver layouts and artifacts can change, and association of an event to a session must not depend on a UI being open. So the device owns session lifecycle and event association.
MobileService creates exactly one active session, enforced by both a partial unique database index and an app-level check, and snapshots the current alive receiver layout at start — essential because enemy receivers can be moved, deleted, or changed later, and the AAR must preserve what the session actually modeled against. Completed Signal Coverage events attach automatically: association runs off boundary hooks after a tx_event is created and again through reconciliation on active-load, detail-load, and end, so an event is recovered even if a realtime hook was missed. Each session event stores a snapshot copy of its transmitter and frequency context, heatmap references, captures, detections, and analysis, with explicit warnings — missing receiver results, missing receiver snapshot, missing heatmap reference, partial analysis — when supporting data is incomplete. The AAR stays valuable even if a source artifact later disappears, and it stays honest about what is missing rather than pretending the evidence is complete. The backend also derives the session summary from those snapshots — outcome counts, receiver exposure, worst event, and warnings — instead of asking the instructor to assemble a report by hand. Ending a session is idempotent, so a retried end never corrupts the original ended state. And while a session is active, Signal Coverage's Clear All is blocked with a typed 409 conflict so the operational event store cannot be wiped out from under a running session.
The client workflow
On the client, Training Session appears as a workflow peer to Planning Mode and Signal Coverage. If no session is active, the instructor can start one; setup surfaces receiver readiness and the snapshot context. Once started, the session stays active even if the panel closes, and a persistent chip keeps it visible. As real events complete they appear in the session timeline, where the instructor can open event detail, pin events, and add notes; ending the session immediately shows the AAR with its summary, timeline, teaching moments, receiver exposure, and notes. The client treats MobileService as authoritative — the active-session REST endpoint is the source of truth, the training_session_changed websocket message is used only as an invalidation signal rather than as complete state, and session event detail is composed from existing Signal Coverage concepts so it feels familiar instead of being a second RF analysis UI.
The bridge across time
Training Session Review is also the bridge between Signal Coverage and Planning Mode, because it is the workflow that understands both time and intent. Signal Coverage is the "actual" side — a real event occurred and was modeled. Planning Mode is the "intended" side — candidate placements were modeled before transmission. The session consumes Signal Coverage events as evidence today, and the preserved north-star direction is to also consume a completed Planning result as a durable plan snapshot, capturing the actual RF inputs, recommendations, receiver outcomes, friendly links, warnings, and selected reference candidate the instructor saw — never an in-memory job ID, because Planning jobs expire. Once that integration lands, the full loop becomes: plan candidate placement, start a session with receiver context and a plan snapshot, execute, let Signal Coverage capture real activity, let the session attach the analyzed events, and let the AAR compare what was planned against what actually happened. Planning makes the intended lower-risk behavior visible before action; Signal Coverage makes the real consequence visible after action; Training Session Review turns both into a teachable story.
Staying honest
Because an AAR can sound authoritative, Training Session enforces the same truthfulness boundaries as the rest of AeroT. It can say that, given configured RF inputs, terrain, and receiver locations, the model predicted likely exposure; that a receiver was modeled as exposed, possible, or not exposed; that an event occurred during the session and was associated with it; that a receiver snapshot was preserved; and that a given result was available, missing, partial, or warning-bound. It does not say a transmission was definitely detected by a real adversary, that a placement is guaranteed safe, that a student passed or failed on a numeric score, that it knows true transmitter power from passive RF, or that it can replace instructor judgment. The backend validation pass confirmed there is no scoring, route-safety, autonomous-coaching, or real-adversary language anywhere in the session code. Training Session Review is a training-continuity feature, not a grading engine.

Technical Deep Dive
Why the Architecture Was Split
AeroT had to operate in disconnected environments, across multiple user surfaces, with hardware that could fail or disappear. A monolithic application would have made that harder. The final architecture split responsibility by runtime boundary:
DeviceManager owns embedded C++ orchestration, SDR drivers, detection, GPS, hardware health, local settings, and signal capture production.
LoRaDriver owns Digi XBee integration and low-bandwidth transport bridging.
Shared owns message contracts and protobuf definitions used across device and command components.
Command View owns the instructor command-node workflow, MQTT subscriptions, device configuration, simulated receiver management, Longley-Rice analysis, heatmaps, and historical timeline UX.
MobileService owns on-device ATAK APIs, on-device RF analysis, local event persistence, and heatmap serving.
ATAK Plugin owns Android/ATAK-native UX, enemy receiver placement, device state, heatmap overlays, timeline interaction, and field instructor interaction.
That split let each service use the best tool for the job. C++ was used where deterministic device control, SDR processing, and RF modeling mattered. Python was used where hardware vendor API support and service composition were strongest. React and MapLibre were used where a web command view made sense. Java and ATAK APIs were used where the workflow had to live inside the tactical map ecosystem.
Signal Event Lifecycle
A signal event travels through AeroT in a deliberate sequence:
An operator configures line-of-sight frequencies, wideband ranges, and modeled transmit power in Command View.
Command View validates the configuration and publishes it to the device.
DeviceManager persists the configuration locally and assigns frequencies to SDR drivers.
The SDR driver samples IQ data through SoapySDR.
The PSD algorithm converts raw IQ samples into a power estimate.
The anomaly detector compares current power behavior to the calibrated baseline distribution.
When a detection starts, DeviceManager emits a signal capture message with a signal UUID.
When the detection ends, DeviceManager emits the matching end capture.
LoRaDriver serializes compact protobuf messages over the Digi XBee transport.
Command View receives and deconflicts the event, then runs RF analysis.
MobileService can perform the same analysis on-device for ATAK clients.
Command View or ATAK visualizes the event as a heatmap, receiver links, and receiver details.
This lifecycle is why AeroT can explain a signal rather than merely detect one. The signal capture is the input. The analysis and visualization are the user value.
Longley-Rice and Terrain-Aware Feedback
AeroT's terrain analysis is built around DTED and Longley-Rice ITM. DTED matters because training terrain is not flat, and receiver visibility is strongly affected by elevation profiles, ridgelines, distance, and path loss. The RF modeling code samples terrain between transmitter and receiver, feeds that profile into Longley-Rice point-to-point analysis, computes path loss, and estimates received power at each simulated receiver.
For heatmaps, the system repeats that analysis across a grid around the transmission location. Each grid point becomes a modeled receiver. The result is normalized into a visual intensity map, encoded as a PNG, and georeferenced by corner coordinates so it can be laid onto MapLibre or ATAK. The command node and field ATAK view therefore share the same conceptual output:
A transmitter at the location where the AeroT device detected the signal.
Enemy receiver sites placed by instructors.
Colored links showing receiver-specific detectability.
A heatmap showing broader signal visibility across the terrain.
Event details that explain frequency, power, receiver power, path loss, distance, and likelihood.
The Transmit Power Modeling Decision
One important technical decision was to make transmit power an explicit configuration input. A passive receiver can measure local received energy. It cannot reliably infer the original transmitter's true output power without a calibrated end-to-end RF chain: transmitter power, impedance, cable losses, antenna gain and pattern, receiver calibration, frequency-specific corrections, terrain, and propagation path. That was outside MVP scope and would have made the product harder to use.
Instead, AeroT lets the instructor configure the modeled TX power level. That keeps the analysis honest. The system is not claiming to magically know the external radio's true transmit power. It is saying: "Given this configured transmitter power and this observed signal event, here is what the adversary receiver model would likely see."
Why ATAK Required On-Device Analysis
The ATAK workflow forced a network architecture decision. The field device could communicate compact messages over the XBee network, but heatmap images and repeated analysis payloads would consume too much bandwidth and degrade the network. The solution was to move heavy analysis to the device for ATAK use.
That is why MobileService exists. It lets the ATAK plugin pull analysis over local Wi-Fi from the AeroT hotspot instead of forcing heavy artifacts through the low-bandwidth radio mesh. Command View can still receive compact captures over the radio network and run command-node analysis, while ATAK clients close to the device get the richer artifacts over direct IP.
One Modeling Path, Three Workflows
A recurring architectural decision across Planning Mode and Training Session Review was to reuse AeroT's existing modeling and visualization rather than fork it. Planning candidate heatmaps call the same extracted terrain-aware generation path as Signal Coverage. Planning result detail and Training Session event detail compose the same assessment and receiver-outcome concepts the operator already knows from Signal Coverage. Even the friendly-link assessment reuses the existing tick-bar container, only swapping in connectivity language. This was a conscious bet: three workflows that share one modeling engine and one visual vocabulary read as a single coherent product, are cheaper to maintain, and keep the analysis honest because every surface explains a signal the same way. The places where the new features intentionally diverge — connectivity vocabulary instead of detection vocabulary, stricter Planning heatmap bounds handling, and temporary planning-* heatmap storage separate from Signal Coverage artifacts — are exactly the places where collapsing them would have misrepresented the modeled result.
Sessions and Plans as Durable State, Not UI State
Training Session Review pushed a boundary that earlier AeroT surfaces did not: durable, multi-client state that has to outlive any single UI. Earlier workflows could treat the client as a fairly stateless view of live device data. A training session cannot — it spans a period of time, multiple clients, and events that complete while no panel is open. That is why the session lives in MobileService with database-enforced single-active-session, receiver and event snapshots, idempotent end, and reconciliation that recovers events a realtime hook might have missed. The same principle drives the deferred Planning integration: when a plan is eventually linked to a session it must be persisted as a snapshot of what the instructor actually saw, never a reference to a Planning job, because Planning jobs are intentionally temporary. The lesson carried from the device stack to these features is the same one that runs through all of AeroT — be explicit about what is authoritative, and do not let important truth depend on something transient.
Engineering Lessons
The hardest part of AeroT was that every layer affected every other layer.
Power regulation looked like random USB instability until we connected it to Raspberry Pi peripheral throttling. Enclosure color and airflow looked like physical design choices until they affected thermal behavior and SDR reliability. GPS looked like a peripheral integration task until internal electromagnetic emissions denied our own receiver. Transport looked like a messaging problem until bandwidth limits forced us to split analysis between command-node and on-device workflows. Signal detection looked like an algorithm problem until field data showed why baseline calibration, retune flushing, and consecutive-sample debouncing mattered.
That is what made AeroT a senior engineering project. The job was not only to write services. It was to keep a hardware/software/RF/user-experience system coherent while every constraint was moving.
The MVP succeeded because we kept the architecture modular, kept the message contracts explicit, kept user feedback central, and kept cutting toward the smallest field-validatable behavior: detect a training-relevant signal, model adversary detectability against terrain, and show the instructor fast enough to change the student's behavior.

Portfolio-Ready Short Version
AeroT was a COTS-based RF training system that I led from thesis concept to MVP. It used Raspberry Pi field devices, SDRs, GPS, Digi XBee transport, C++ signal processing, Python hardware integration, Longley-Rice terrain modeling, React/MapLibre Command View, and an ATAK plugin to give instructors real-time electromagnetic discipline feedback during live training. The device detected configured RF activity, modeled whether simulated adversarial receivers could see the transmission across DTED terrain, and displayed heatmaps and receiver links in Command View and ATAK. The final validation connected ten devices across roughly ten miles of 29 Palms terrain and delivered instructor feedback within about five seconds of signal capture.
Beyond that MVP, I led the next phase on the ATAK plugin, which extended AeroT from after-the-fact analysis into a full instructional loop. Planning Mode added pre-execution RF planning — candidate friendly placements scored for both adversary detectability and friendly-to-friendly connectivity (Viable, Marginal, Unreliable), with terrain-aware candidate heatmaps that reuse the Signal Coverage generation path and live, candidate-level generation progress. Training Session Review added a session-level training loop in which MobileService is the source of truth: an instructor starts a session, the system snapshots the receiver layout, real analyzed events attach automatically, teaching moments are pinned and annotated, and ending the session produces an immediate after-action review. Together they make AeroT a prepare → execute → review loop while keeping the language modeled, assumption-aware, and instructor-led.
